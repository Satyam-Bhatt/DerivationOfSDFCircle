Exterior points — squash to a circle

For any point outside the ellipse, ω₀ comes from a cheap trick: divide x by a and y by b. That maps the ellipse onto the unit circle, and on a circle the closest point to any external point is trivial — it's just the point normalized. Converting that angle back gives ω₀ = atan2(a·y, b·x). It isn't the true closest-point angle on the ellipse, but it's close enough that five Newton steps converge cleanly from there.

Try dragging p far from the ellipse — the yellow seed point and the green converged point will already sit close together, since the seed is a good approximation out there.

Interior points — why the evolute matters

Every point on the ellipse has a normal line running through it. The evolute is the curve traced by where those normal lines pile up on each other — technically, the locus of centers of curvature. For an ellipse it's an astroid-like curve with four sharp cusps, sitting on the two axes at (±(a²−b²)/a, 0) and (0, ±(a²−b²)/b).

Points strictly inside the ellipse but near this curve are where the "which boundary point is closest" question stops having one obvious answer — nearby interior points can have their closest boundary point suddenly jump from near the (a, 0) vertex to near the (0, b) vertex. A single formula for ω₀ can't track that jump, so Newton needs to be told in advance which side it's on.

The code uses a cheap stand-in for that boundary: a straight line through the point (a, b) with slope a/b — shown in orange-brown above. It isn't the exact evolute boundary, just a line close enough to it that it reliably drops the seed on the correct side. Points on one side start at ω₀ = 0 (seeded near the a-vertex); the other side starts at ω₀ = π/2 (seeded near the b-vertex).

Turn on "Newton path" and drag p slowly across that orange line — you'll see the seed point (yellow) jump between the two ellipse vertices even though p barely moved, while the actual converged point (green) moves smoothly. That discontinuity in the seed is fine: it's a starting guess, not the answer.
